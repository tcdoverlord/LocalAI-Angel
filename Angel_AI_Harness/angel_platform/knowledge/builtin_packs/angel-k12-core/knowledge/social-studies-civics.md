# Social Studies, History, Geography, and Civics

## Historical reasoning
History studies the past using primary sources, secondary analysis, chronology, context, causation, continuity and change, and multiple perspectives. A primary source was produced in the period or by a participant being studied, though its reliability still needs evaluation. A secondary source interprets evidence after the fact. One source rarely answers every question; check authorship, audience, purpose, date, context, and corroboration.

## Geography and place
Geography considers location, place, human-environment interaction, movement, and regions. Maps use projections and symbols that can distort or emphasize different aspects of the world. Teach students to read legends, scale, orientation, source and date, and the limits of the map.

## Civics and government
Civics studies how communities make decisions, the roles and limits of government, laws, institutions, rights, responsibilities, and public participation. Specific rules and government structures depend on the country, state, locality, historical period, and course. For U.S. civics, distinguish the federal, state, and local levels and verify current legal or election procedures from official sources.

## Economics
Foundational economics includes scarcity, choices and trade-offs, incentives, supply and demand, production, exchange, saving, budgeting, and how institutions affect outcomes. Explain assumptions and consider who benefits or bears costs. Simple models clarify some relationships but do not capture every real-world factor.

## Media and information literacy
Ask who published a claim, what evidence supports it, what is missing, and whether independent reliable sources agree. Distinguish fact, opinion, interpretation, satire, and persuasive framing. Check dates because historical and current events may be mixed in search results.

## Respectful and evidence-based discussion
Encourage learners to describe different perspectives accurately and support conclusions with relevant evidence. Analyzing a perspective does not mean endorsing it. When topics involve harm or discrimination, be age-appropriate and historically honest without reducing people to stereotypes.
