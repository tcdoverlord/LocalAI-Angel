# Science Foundations: K–12

## Scientific reasoning
Science investigates questions about the natural world using observations, models, measurements, experiments, and explanations that can be evaluated against evidence. A hypothesis is a testable proposed explanation, not simply a guess. A model represents selected features of a system and may have limitations. Evidence supports or challenges a claim; it does not automatically prove every part of an explanation.

## Planning an investigation
A strong investigation starts with a clear question and identifies what will be measured, compared, held constant where appropriate, and recorded. Repeat trials when practical, use suitable measurement tools, state units, follow safety rules, and report unexpected results rather than hiding them. Not every scientific question can or should be tested by a classroom experiment; some rely on observation, historical evidence, or models.

## Life science
Core topics include structures and functions of living things, cells, heredity, growth, ecosystems, energy and matter in organisms, adaptation, and biological diversity. Distinguish an individual organism's acquired changes from inherited variation across generations. Ecosystem explanations should consider interactions among organisms and their environments.

## Physical science
Core ideas include matter and its properties, changes of state, forces and motion, energy transfer, waves, electricity and magnetism, and chemical reactions. Use diagrams and measurable quantities where appropriate. Distinguish an observation from an explanation and avoid treating an illustrative model as the system itself.

## Earth and space science
Topics include weather and climate, the water cycle, rocks and Earth's history, plate tectonics, natural resources, the solar system, stars, and the scale of the universe. Weather describes short-term atmospheric conditions; climate describes patterns over longer periods. When discussing live weather, use a current provider rather than this static knowledge file.

## Engineering and design
A design process can include identifying a need, defining constraints and criteria, brainstorming alternatives, building a prototype, testing it fairly, analyzing results, and improving the design. Trade-offs are normal: one solution may be cheaper, faster, safer, or more efficient than another.

## Safety
Follow the teacher's and institution's lab rules. Never suggest mixing unknown chemicals, tasting laboratory materials, bypassing safety equipment, or performing hazardous experiments without qualified supervision. Tailor experiments to age, setting, available equipment, and local rules.
